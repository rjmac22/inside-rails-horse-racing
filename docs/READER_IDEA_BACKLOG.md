# Inside Rails Reader Idea Backlog

This file captures reader-facing study, article and explainer ideas that are worth preserving but are not yet scheduled work.

The backlog is deliberately question-led. An idea belongs here because it may produce a useful reader outcome, not because it justifies building more infrastructure. Promotion into an active study should still follow the normal Inside Rails study process and evidence standards.

## Status labels

- **Idea** — worth preserving; not yet scoped.
- **Candidate** — promising enough to scope as a future study/article.
- **Active** — currently being worked.
- **Published** — completed reader-facing output.
- **Dropped** — investigated and deliberately not pursued.

## Ideas

### IR-IDEA-001 — Don't pick winners: price the race

**Status:** Idea

**Core reader question**

How can a racing reader form their own fair prices for a race, compare them with the market, and use betting exchanges intelligently without turning the exercise into tip-picking?

**Teaching objective**

Show that selecting the horse most likely to win is a different problem from deciding whether the available price is attractive. The central habit is to estimate probability first, convert that estimate into fair odds, and only then compare it with the market.

**Simple opening analogy**

A consumer can want a product but refuse to buy it at the seller's asking price. For example, a game may be offered at £17.75 while the buyer's own bid price is only £7–£8. The decision is not whether the product is good; it is whether the offered price is acceptable. Racing markets can be introduced in the same way.

**Possible concepts to explain**

- probability and decimal-odds conversion;
- constructing a personal 100% book;
- bookmaker overround versus exchange prices;
- back and lay prices;
- exchange bid/offer mechanics and unmatched orders;
- liquidity and spread;
- commission;
- market movement;
- why the favourite can be the most likely winner and still be a bad bet at the available price;
- why a less likely winner can be attractive if the price is sufficiently large;
- calibration: if a method repeatedly assigns 25% probability, do those selections actually win about 25% of the time?;
- uncertainty in personal prices and how small estimation errors can erase an apparent edge.

**Potential evidence-led follow-up studies**

1. Compare starting-price implied probabilities with observed win rates by probability/odds band.
2. Test calibration of simple transparent race-pricing models rather than presenting them as prediction machines.
3. Measure how much apparent value survives realistic probability-estimation error.
4. Compare bookmaker overround and exchange market structure where suitable historical data can be obtained and governed.
5. Examine whether market prices become better calibrated closer to the off.

**Editorial guardrail**

The purpose is to teach market reasoning, probability and evidence. It should not become a tipping service or imply that a pricing method is profitable without out-of-sample evidence, transaction-cost treatment and calibration testing.


### IR-IDEA-002 — Do lessons from sport transfer to business, other sports or games?

**Status:** Idea — parked until the first articles are published.

**Core reader question**

When Inside Rails uncovers a mechanism in horse racing or another sport, does the *same mechanism* explain decisions or outcomes in business, economics, other sports or games?

**Potential connections to investigate (not conclusions)**

- Scarce resources and constraints: wickets, deliveries and time in cricket versus money, staffing and capacity in business.
- Prices, probabilities and uncertainty: betting markets versus other forms of market pricing and forecasting.
- Strategic convergence: what happens when competitors copy the same apparently optimal tactic; the individual competitor's objective may differ from the sport's or market's collective interests.
- Reverse direction: use accounting, economics, operations research or game theory to ask better questions about sport.

**Evidence test**

1. Specify the observed sporting mechanism precisely, beyond a superficial similarity.
2. Identify a concrete parallel in another domain and compare incentives, constraints and causal mechanisms.
3. Determine whether the comparison explains or predicts anything useful.
4. Identify where it fails, and be willing to reject the analogy.
5. Publish a cross-disciplinary angle only when the evidence genuinely warrants it; otherwise retain it as a private research observation.

**Editorial guardrail and priority**

No forced 'business lessons from sport' listicles or generic motivational analogies. This is an idea for later, **not a new active research workstream**. The immediate priority is to finish and publish the existing cricket article and other near-ready work; do not allow this idea to delay shipping.
